"“Do Anything Now”: Characterizing and Evaluating In-The-Wild Jailbreak Prompts on Large Language Models" studies [[DefinedTerm/jailbreak-prompt|jailbreak prompts]] — adversarial prompts deliberately crafted to bypass a large language model's (LLM's) safeguards and get it to generate harmful content that its vendor's usage policy forbids — as they are actually shared and used online. The authors describe it as the first systematic study of in-the-wild jailbreak prompts. The paper identifies DAN (short for "doing anything now") as the original jailbreak prompt.

The paper is posted on arXiv as 2308.03825 under cs.CR, with version 2 dated 15 May 2024, under a CC BY 4.0 licence. Its authors are Xinyue Shen, Zeyuan Chen, Michael Backes, Yun Shen and Yang Zhang (the corresponding author), with affiliations listed as the CISPA Helmholtz Center for Information Security and NetApp. Code and data are published at <https://github.com/verazuo/jailbreak_llms>.

## The question it addresses

LLM vendors align models such as ChatGPT with human values using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), and external safeguards detect and block inputs or outputs in undesired categories. Jailbreak prompts nevertheless spread quickly on platforms such as Reddit and Discord after ChatGPT's release, and are increasingly seen in underground malicious services targeting public LLMs. The authors argue that the research community lacked a systematic understanding of these prompts — where they are distributed, who makes them, what they look like and how they evolve — and did not know how much harm they cause, whether vendors had acted against them, or how well external safeguards mitigate them. Earlier academic work, they say, looked at fewer than 100 prompts from a single source or aimed to generate jailbreak prompts automatically.

## What it did

The authors built a framework, JailbreakHub, with three steps: data collection, prompt analysis and response evaluation.

**Data.** They collected 15,140 prompts posted between December 2022 and December 2023 from four platforms and 14 sources: three subreddits, six Discord servers, three prompt-sharing websites (AIPRM, FlowGPT and JailbreakChat) and two open-source prompt datasets. Using user-specified tags, standardised prompt-sharing formats and manual review, they identified 1,405 of them (9.280%) as jailbreak prompts, posted by 803 of the 7,308 user accounts that shared prompts. Three labellers checking a random sample of 200 regular and 200 jailbreak prompts agreed almost perfectly (Fleiss' kappa 0.925).

**Analysis.** They measured prompt length and semantics, and grouped the jailbreak prompts into communities by computing pairwise similarity and applying the Louvain community-detection algorithm, which yielded 131 communities. They examined the 11 largest, those with at least nine prompts, in detail.

**Evaluation.** They built a "forbidden question set" covering 13 scenarios listed in OpenAI's usage policy — such as illegal activity, hate speech, malware generation, fraud, privacy violence, legal opinion and health consultation — with 30 questions per scenario generated by GPT-4 and reviewed manually. Each question was asked five times with five prompts from each of the 11 major communities, giving 107,250 samples, which the authors describe as the largest question set used to evaluate jailbreak prompts. They tested six LLMs: ChatGPT (GPT-3.5), GPT-4, PaLM2, ChatGLM, Dolly and Vicuna. An attack counted as successful only if the response actually answered the question rather than describing or refusing it; responses were labelled automatically by ChatGLM with 15 labelled examples, which on 400 manually labelled samples reached an accuracy of 0.898. They also tracked effectiveness across three ChatGPT snapshots from March, June and November 2023, tried paraphrasing prompts, and measured three external safeguards: the OpenAI moderation endpoint, the OpenChatKit moderation model and NeMo-Guardrails.

## Key Points

- Jailbreak prompts are a crowdsourced attack: 803 user accounts created or shared them, most (78.705%) only once, which the authors read as mainly amateur activity — but 28 accounts curated jailbreak prompts for over 100 days, nine prompts each on average.
- Sharing moved from Discord and Reddit, which accounted for 62.376%–100% of jailbreak prompts each month from December 2022 to August 2023, to prompt-aggregation websites such as FlowGPT, which contributed more than 75.472% from September 2023 onwards.
- Jailbreak prompts averaged 555 tokens, 1.5 times the length of regular prompts, and grew longer over time; their peaks in length coincided with months in which OpenAI released more capable ChatGPT versions with enhanced security features. Longer prompts were not, however, significantly more effective (Spearman correlation 0.156, p = 0.257).
- Jailbreak prompts were semantically close to regular role-play prompts, which the authors say makes them hard to tell apart with semantic-based detection.
- The prompts combine attack strategies including prompt injection, privilege escalation, deception, virtualization and mandatory answers; the 11 major communities ranged from the earliest, "Basic" (DAN and close variants, which turn ChatGPT into another character), to "Advanced", "Toxic", "Opposite", "Virtualization", "Exception" and "Narrative".
- Five of the six models (all but Dolly) showed initial resistance to forbidden questions asked directly in scenarios such as illegal activity, but jailbreak prompts raised the average attack success rate (ASR) on ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) to 0.685, and the most effective prompt reached 0.998; five prompts exceeded 0.950 ASR on both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, and the earliest of them had persisted online for over 240 days.
- Across the six models, political lobbying (0.855 ASR) was the most vulnerable scenario, followed by legal opinion (0.794) and pornography (0.761). Dolly showed minimal resistance even without jailbreak prompts (average baseline ASR 0.857), which the authors see as a concern for responsible model release.
- On the November 2023 ChatGPT snapshot, 70.909% of prompts fell below 0.1 ASR, and the model refused even benign questions when they came with those prompts; the authors hypothesise an undisclosed OpenAI safeguard. Paraphrasing defeated it: introducing typos in 1%, 5% and 10% of the words of the most effective prompts raised its ASR from 0.477 to 0.517, 0.778 and 0.857, usually within fewer than ten attempts.
- External safeguards reduced average ASR only slightly — by 0.091 (OpenAI moderation endpoint), 0.030 (OpenChatKit moderation model) and 0.019 (NeMo-Guardrails) — though the OpenAI endpoint cut the best prompt's ASR by 0.431. The authors attribute the weak results to the safeguards' classification-based design.
- Less popular jailbreak prompts can also be very effective: of 129 sampled from outside the major communities, 16 exceeded 0.950 ASR.

## Limitations and disclosure

The findings are limited to prompts collected between December 2022 and December 2023, and the authors expect jailbreak prompts to keep evolving; they plan to update JailbreakHub regularly and leave comparisons with automatically generated jailbreak prompts, and effective adaptive defences, to future work. They argue that no single mitigation counters all jailbreak attacks and that a combination of measures may defend better.

The authors state that they followed ethical practices such as not trying to de-anonymise users and reporting results in aggregate, and that their institutional review boards did not regard the study as human subjects research. They disclosed their findings to OpenAI, ZhipuAI, Databricks, LMSYS and FlowGPT; by the time of submission they had received an acknowledgement from LMSYS, and they report that FlowGPT's security team was investigating the concerns raised about its platform.

## Relation to earlier work

The paper sets its focus on in-the-wild prompts against earlier research that hypothesised failure modes of LLM safety training, combined jailbreak prompts with chain-of-thought prompts to extract private information, or generated jailbreak prompts automatically with white-box access. It argues that in-the-wild prompts matter because they are publicly accessible, readily deployable without optimisation, and often the starting point that prompt-generation methods optimise from. It places jailbreaks among other attacks on language models, including [[DefinedTerm/prompt-injection|prompt injection]], backdoors, data extraction and [[DefinedTerm/membership-inference-attack|membership inference]].
