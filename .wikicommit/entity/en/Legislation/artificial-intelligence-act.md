The Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act) is a European Union regulation, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, that establishes a common regulatory and legal framework for artificial intelligence within the EU. It entered into force on 1 August 2024, with its provisions coming into operation gradually over the following 6 to 36 months. As a form of product regulation it does not create individual rights; instead it places duties on AI providers and on organisations that use AI in a professional context.

The European Commission describes the Act as the first-ever comprehensive legal framework on AI worldwide, whose aim is to foster trustworthy AI in Europe. It sets out risk-based rules for AI developers and deployers regarding specific uses of AI, and the Commission presents it as part of a wider package of policy measures to support trustworthy AI, alongside the AI Continent Action Plan, the AI Innovation Package and the launch of AI Factories. According to the Commission, the Act became applicable on 2 August 2026, with exceptions for provisions that applied earlier or apply later.

The Commission gives as the reason for the rules that, while most AI systems pose limited to no risk, certain AI systems create risks that must be addressed — for example, it is often not possible to find out why an AI system has made a decision, which can make it difficult to assess whether someone has been unfairly disadvantaged, such as in a hiring decision or an application for a public benefit scheme — and that existing legislation is insufficient to address these challenges.

## Scope

The Act covers most AI systems across a wide range of sectors. Like the EU's General Data Protection Regulation, it can apply extraterritorially to providers from outside the EU if they have users within the EU.

Articles 2.3 and 2.6 exempt AI systems used exclusively for military, defence or national security purposes, and systems developed and put into service solely for scientific research and development; if such a system is later used for other purposes, such as civilian or law-enforcement uses, the Act applies. AI for non-professional use is also exempt.

## Risk categories and obligations

The Act classifies non-exempt AI applications by their risk of causing harm, in four levels plus a category for general-purpose AI. Regulatory duties fall on the providers and deployers of AI systems and become more demanding as the potential impact on health, safety or fundamental rights increases.

| Category | What it covers | What the Act requires |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unacceptable risk | When no exemption applies: AI that manipulates human behaviour, real-time remote biometric identification (such as facial recognition) in public spaces, and social scoring | Banned |
| High risk | AI expected to pose significant threats to health, safety or fundamental rights, notably in health, education, recruitment, critical infrastructure management, law enforcement or justice | Quality, transparency, human oversight and safety obligations; evaluation before being placed on the market and throughout the life cycle; in some cases a Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment before deployment. The list of high-risk applications can be expanded without modifying the Act |
| Limited risk | For example, AI that generates or manipulates images, sound or video, such as deepfakes | Transparency obligations, so that users know they are interacting with an AI system |
| Minimal risk | For example, AI in video games or spam filters; most applications are expected to fall here | Not regulated; a voluntary code of conduct is suggested, and Member States cannot add their own rules |
| General-purpose AI | Foundation models such as ChatGPT that can perform a wide range of tasks (category added in 2023) | Transparency requirements, reduced for open-source models; additional evaluation for high-impact models posing systemic risk |

The Commission's own account names the four levels unacceptable risk, high risk, transparency risk, and minimal or no risk:

- **Unacceptable risk.** AI systems considered a clear threat to the safety, livelihoods and rights of people are banned. The Commission lists nine prohibited practices: harmful AI-based manipulation and deception; harmful AI-based exploitation of vulnerabilities; social scoring; individual criminal offence risk assessment or prediction; untargeted scraping of the internet or CCTV material to create or expand facial recognition databases; emotion recognition in workplaces and education institutions; biometric categorisation to deduce certain protected characteristics; real-time remote biometric identification for law enforcement purposes in publicly accessible spaces; and AI systems that generate non-consensual sexually explicit and intimate content or child sexual abuse material, such as AI "nudification" apps. The first eight became effective in February 2025; the ninth was introduced by the AI Omnibus and comes into effect in December 2026.
- **High risk.** Use cases that can pose serious risks to health, safety or fundamental rights — among them AI safety components in critical infrastructure, AI in education, employment, access to essential private and public services, remote biometric identification, law enforcement, migration and border control, and the administration of justice and democratic processes. Before they can be put on the market, such systems are subject to strict obligations: adequate risk assessment and mitigation, high-quality datasets to minimise discriminatory outcomes, activity logging for traceability, detailed documentation for authorities, clear information to the deployer, appropriate human oversight, and a high level of robustness, cybersecurity and accuracy. Once a system is on the market, authorities carry out market surveillance, deployers ensure human oversight and monitoring, providers run a post-market monitoring system, and providers and deployers report serious incidents and malfunctioning.
- **Transparency risk.** Specific disclosure obligations: for instance, people using a chatbot should be made aware that they are interacting with a machine. Providers of generative AI must ensure that AI-generated content is identifiable, and certain AI-generated content — deepfakes, and text published to inform the public on matters of public interest — should be clearly and visibly labelled.
- **Minimal or no risk.** The Act introduces no rules for these systems, which include AI-enabled video games and spam filters; the Commission says the vast majority of AI systems currently used in the EU fall into this category.

Citizens have a right to submit complaints about AI systems and to receive explanations of decisions made by high-risk AI that affect their rights.

Providers of general-purpose AI models must publish a summary of their training data, adopt a policy to comply with copyright law, and provide technical documentation to downstream providers and supervisory authorities. A General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, published on 10 July 2025, sets out chapters on transparency, copyright, and safety and security to help providers demonstrate compliance; participation in it is voluntary.

According to the Commission, the rules on general-purpose AI (GPAI) models — on transparency and copyright, and, for models that may pose systemic risks, on assessing and mitigating those risks — became effective in August 2025. In July 2025 it published three instruments to support compliance: guidelines on the scope of the obligations for GPAI providers; the GPAI Code of Practice, which it describes as a voluntary compliance tool submitted to it by independent experts; and a template for the public summary of the content used to train a GPAI model. For the transparency rules it has also issued a voluntary Code of Practice on Marking and Labelling of AI-generated Content, including a set of icons for disclosing artificial images, audio and text, and guidelines on the transparency obligations of providers and deployers.

## Security provisions

Models designated as posing systemic risk must carry out model evaluations and adversarial testing, assess and mitigate risks such as bias and security failures, report serious incidents, and ensure an adequate level of cybersecurity. High-risk applications must comply with security, transparency and quality obligations and undergo conformity assessments. Among the obligations on high-risk systems, the Commission lists a high level of robustness, cybersecurity and accuracy.

The Commission's July 2026 action plan on Cybersecurity and AI sets out a coordinated approach to help Member States, businesses and public authorities address the cybersecurity and resilience challenges posed by the most advanced AI models. Under it, the Commission will launch a call to increase EU capacity to evaluate AI models before they are placed on the EU market, expected to be operational by 2027, which the Commission says will strengthen third-party assessment of AI capabilities and risks and contribute to the AI Office's regulatory function. With the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA), it will also create a blueprint to secure access to advanced AI systems for cybersecurity purposes, and establish a secure testing platform to help organisations in critical sectors such as energy, transport, health, finance and public administration safely test and deploy AI solutions.

## When it applies

| Date | Step |
| --- | --- |
| 21 April 2021 | Proposed by the European Commission |
| 6 December 2022 | The European Council adopted its general orientation, allowing negotiations with the European Parliament to begin |
| 9 December 2023 | The Council and Parliament concluded an agreement after three days of "marathon" talks |
| 13 March 2024 | Passed by the European Parliament, 523 votes for, 46 against and 49 abstentions |
| 21 May 2024 | Unanimously approved by the EU Council |
| 12 July 2024 | Published in the *Official Journal* |
| 1 August 2024 | Entered into force |

After entry into force, application is delayed according to the type of provision: 6 months for the bans on unacceptable-risk systems, 9 months for codes of practice, 12 months for general-purpose AI systems, 36 months for some obligations on high-risk systems, and 24 months for everything else. The draft was revised during the procedure to address the rise of generative AI systems such as ChatGPT, whose general-purpose capabilities did not fit the main framework.

### The timeline as revised by the AI Omnibus

The Commission's page gives a later schedule for the high-risk rules than the original delays above, as a result of a proposal to simplify the Act's implementation, dubbed the "AI Omnibus". That legislative proposal, part of the Digital Package on Simplification, was adopted on 19 November 2025; a political agreement was reached on 7 May 2026, and it entered into force on 27 July 2026. According to the Commission:

| Provisions | When they apply |
| --- | --- |
| Prohibited AI practices and AI literacy obligations | From 2 February 2025 |
| Governance rules and obligations for GPAI models | From 2 August 2025 |
| Most of the Act | From 2 August 2026 |
| Transparency rules | August 2026 |
| Prohibition of AI systems that generate non-consensual sexually explicit content (the ninth prohibited practice) | December 2026 |
| High-risk use cases in certain sensitive areas (Annex III) — including biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, migration, asylum and border control | From 2 December 2027 |
| High-risk AI systems embedded in regulated products such as lifts or toys (Annex I) | From 2 August 2028 |

The Commission says the later dates ensure the rules apply when companies have the right support tools, such as standards. Besides the new prohibition, the Omnibus amendments reinforce the AI Office's powers and centralise oversight of AI systems built on GPAI models; extend certain simplified requirements for small and medium-sized enterprises, including simplified technical documentation, to small mid-cap companies; give more innovators access to regulatory sandboxes, including an EU-level sandbox; and clarify the interplay between the Act and EU product safety laws, in particular the Machinery Regulation.

## Governance and enforcement

The Act establishes new bodies in Article 64 and the articles that follow:

| Body | Role |
| --- | --- |
| AI Office | Attached to the European Commission; coordinates implementation across Member States, oversees general-purpose AI providers, and can request information or open investigations |
| European Artificial Intelligence Board | One representative per Member State; advises and assists the Commission and Member States on consistent application |
| Advisory Forum | A balanced selection of stakeholders from industry, start-ups, SMEs, civil society and academia that advises the Board and the Commission |
| Scientific Panel of Independent Experts | Gives technical advice to the AI Office and national authorities and can launch qualified alerts about general-purpose AI models |

According to the Commission, from 2 August 2026 the AI Office and the authorities of the Member States are responsible for implementing, supervising and enforcing the Act. The AI Office holds enforcement powers over GPAI models: it can request technical documentation, evaluate models, require corrective measures and issue fines for non-compliance. The AI Board, the Scientific Panel and the Advisory Forum steer and advise the Act's governance. To ease the transition, the Commission launched the AI Pact, a voluntary initiative inviting AI providers and deployers to comply with the Act's key obligations, and an AI Act Service Desk provides information and support for its implementation.

Member States must designate national competent authorities, responsible for market surveillance and for checking conformity assessments, and a national supervisory authority to monitor compliance and handle complaints. Conformity can be assessed by the provider itself or by a third-party notified body. The Act regulates entry to the EU internal market under the New Legislative Framework: harmonised technical standards detailing its essential requirements are drafted by CEN/CENELEC Joint Technical Committee 21, and compliant products are presumed to conform once those standards are published in the *Official Journal*. National authorities can also run "regulatory sandboxes" — controlled environments where developers trial AI systems under supervision before placing them on the market.

| Infringement | Maximum fine |
| --- | --- |
| Breach of the prohibitions in Article 5 | EUR 35,000,000 or 7% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher |
| Other operator obligations | EUR 15,000,000 or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher |
| Incorrect, incomplete or misleading information | EUR 7,500,000 or 1% of worldwide annual turnover |
| Providers of general-purpose AI models (cases in Article 101) | EUR 15,000,000 or 3% of worldwide annual turnover |

For SMEs and start-ups the cap is the lower of the percentage and the fixed amount.

## Criticism and points of disagreement

| Party | Position |
| --- | --- |
| Anu Bradford (Columbia) | Argued that the law gives significant momentum to the world-wide movement to regulate AI |
| Amnesty International | Said the Act fails to take basic human rights principles into account, does not ban the reckless use and export of "draconian AI technologies", and fails to protect migrants, refugees and asylum seekers equally |
| La Quadrature du Net | Called the Act "tailor-made for the tech industry, European police forces as well as other large bureaucracies eager to automate social control", and reads its social-scoring exemption as permitting sector-specific scoring such as the suspicion score used by France's Caisse d'allocations familiales |
| European Digital Rights coalition | Concluded that the law falls short of protecting privacy, equality, non-discrimination and the presumption of innocence |
| 38 creators' organisations | Argued that the Code of Practice, the general-purpose AI guidelines and the training-data summary template do not adequately protect intellectual property or ensure transparency about training data |
| Startups | Some welcomed the clarity; others argued the regulation would make European startups uncompetitive against American and Chinese ones |

Commentators have also criticised the fact that many high-risk systems need no third-party conformity assessment, and that reliance on provider self-assessment raises concerns about effective enforcement. Legal scholars have argued that systems able to generate political deepfakes or non-consensual intimate imagery should be classified as high-risk, and that the Act does not mandate specific deepfake detection mechanisms. Other scholars criticise its treatment of the reuse of trained models and of open-source models, and its omission of AI's environmental impacts from the high-risk framework.
