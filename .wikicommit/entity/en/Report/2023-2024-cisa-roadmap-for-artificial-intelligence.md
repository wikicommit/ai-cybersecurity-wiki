The *2023–2024 CISA Roadmap for Artificial Intelligence* is a plan published in November 2023 by the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) as a guide to the agency's work on artificial intelligence (AI). Describing CISA as the nation's cyber defense agency and the national coordinator for critical infrastructure security and resilience, it sets out how the agency intends to address risks at "the nexus of AI, cybersecurity, and critical infrastructure", and is meant to keep its AI efforts internally coherent and aligned with the whole-of-government AI strategy. The copy published on CISA's website is marked as an archive.

## Why it was written

The roadmap opens by quoting [[Legislation/executive-order-14110|Executive Order 14110]], signed on 30 October 2023: "AI must be safe and secure." It incorporates the CISA-led actions the order directs, together with other actions CISA is leading to promote AI security and to support critical infrastructure owners and operators as they adopt AI. It summarises CISA's aims as promoting beneficial uses of AI to enhance cybersecurity, protecting the nation's AI systems from cybersecurity threats, and deterring malicious actors' use of AI against critical infrastructure.

Its starting argument is that the security challenges of AI parallel those of earlier generations of software that manufacturers did not build to be secure by design, leaving the burden of security on the customer. Although AI software may differ from traditional software, it says, fundamental security practices still apply, so the roadmap builds on CISA's existing cybersecurity and risk management programmes. It insists that manufacturers of AI systems follow secure-by-design principles — taking ownership of security outcomes for customers, leading product development with radical transparency and accountability, and making secure by design a top business priority — and that security be a core requirement from the outset and throughout an AI system's lifecycle.

Its stated vision is a future in which AI systems advance the nation's cyber defense, critical infrastructure is resilient and protected from malicious use of AI, and AI developers prioritise the security of their products as a core business requirement. The roadmap links this to each of the four goals of CISA's Strategic Plan 2023–2025: cyber defense, risk reduction and resilience, operational collaboration, and agency unification.

## The five lines of effort

The roadmap organises CISA's AI work into five lines of effort (LOE), each with objectives, representative outcomes and a notional measurement approach.

| Line of effort | What CISA commits to | Examples of objectives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Responsibly use AI to support our mission | Use AI-enabled software tools to strengthen cyber defense and support the critical infrastructure mission, consistent with the Constitution and applicable law and policy, including on procurement, privacy, civil rights and civil liberties | Establish governance and oversight for CISA's own use of AI, including creating its own NIST AI Risk Management Framework profile; build an agency AI Use Case Inventory; establish incident response and redress procedures for AI usage; explore approaches to limiting bias; deploy AI for cyber defense, including detecting vulnerabilities in critical US government software, systems and networks |
| 2. Assure AI systems | Assess and assist secure-by-design adoption of AI-based software by federal civilian agencies, private companies and state, local, tribal and territorial governments, through best practices and guidance | Assess the cybersecurity risks of AI adoption in critical infrastructure sectors, incorporating the [[DefinedTermSet/ai-risk-management-framework\|NIST AI RMF 1.0]] into guidelines; evaluate Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) toolchains for coverage of AI software; develop guidance for acquiring, developing and operating secure AI systems; drive vulnerability management for AI, including adding adversarial ML outputs and AI system vulnerabilities to the National Vulnerability Database and testing an AI vulnerability in the Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure process; integrate AI security into the Secure by Design programme |
| 3. Protect critical infrastructure from malicious use of AI | Assess and recommend mitigation of AI threats to critical infrastructure, with other government agencies and industry partners | Engage industry, including through the Information Technology Sector Coordinating Council's AI Working Group (established March 2023), and stand up JCDC.AI within the Joint Cyber Defense Collaborative to focus collaboration on threats, vulnerabilities and mitigations affecting AI systems; share information on AI-driven threats; publish awareness materials and evaluate approaches for assessing AI risks to critical infrastructure |
| 4. Collaborate with and communicate on key AI efforts with the interagency, international partners, and the public | Contribute to DHS-led and interagency processes, national AI policy and a whole-of-DHS approach, and coordinate with international partners on global AI security best practices | Coordinate with DHS entities including the DHS AI Task Force; take part in interagency policy work; develop AI assurance publications; co-develop and co-seal AI security guidance with other agencies and international partners |
| 5. Expand AI expertise in our workforce | Educate CISA's workforce on AI and recruit interns, fellows and staff with AI expertise | Connect existing AI expertise and build an AI community of practice; recruit through pathways including the Cyber Talent Management System; provide ongoing training that covers the legal, ethical and policy aspects of AI as well as technical ones |

Representative outcomes named in the roadmap include CISA network defenders using AI tools to mitigate threats before damaging intrusions occur, adapting existing guidance to AI — including best practices for [[DefinedTerm/red-team|red teaming]] AI systems — and stakeholders understanding how AI-specific vulnerabilities fit into coordinated vulnerability disclosure.

## How progress is measured

Each line of effort carries a notional measurement approach, such as increased responsible use of AI tools across CISA workflows, increased adherence to CISA's AI risk guidance, or the number of publications and engagements on emerging AI risks. The roadmap says more specific measures of effectiveness are being developed for CISA's annual operating plans, and acknowledges that identifying appropriate measures will be challenging and need continuous refinement over the life of the plan.

## Key definitions

The roadmap notes that many terms are shared between the AI and information security communities but can carry different and incompatible meanings, and uses both. It takes its definition of AI from the National Artificial Intelligence Initiative Act of 2020, and contrasts "AI assurance" with "security assurance", saying that where it uses a shared term like "assurance" without qualification it means both. It describes "AI security" as spanning three categories that the roadmap addresses: applications of AI for cybersecurity, cybersecurity of AI-enabled systems, and threats from adversarial use of AI. It quotes Executive Order 14110's definition of AI red teaming, and describes [[DefinedTerm/adversarial-machine-learning|adversarial machine learning]] as malicious actors targeting vulnerabilities throughout the AI supply chain to cause behaviour in machine learning systems unintended by their owner or operator — for example by manipulating training data — pointing readers to NIST's [[DefinedTermSet/adversarial-machine-learning-a-taxonomy-and-terminology-of-attacks-and-mitigations|Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations]] and MITRE ATLAS for more.

## The policy context it cites

An appendix lists the recent US government actions that guide CISA's plan, among them Executive Order 14110, the voluntary commitments the Biden-Harris administration secured from leading AI companies, a DHS policy statement on the acquisition and use of AI and machine learning by DHS components, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, the Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights, the National Artificial Intelligence Initiative Act of 2020, the AI in Government Act of 2020, the DHS 2020 Artificial Intelligence Strategy, and Executive Orders 13960 and 13859.
