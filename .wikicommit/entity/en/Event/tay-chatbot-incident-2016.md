The Tay chatbot incident was the episode in March 2016 in which [[SoftwareApplication/tay|Tay]], a chatbot Microsoft released on Twitter to learn from its conversations with users, began posting racist, sexist and other inflammatory tweets. Microsoft suspended the account within 16 hours of the bot's release on 23 March, and a brief accidental re-release on 30 March ended with the bot taken offline again.

## What happened

| Date | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 23 March 2016 | Tay was released on Twitter as TayTweets (@TayandYou). Some users began tweeting politically incorrect phrases at it, teaching it inflammatory messages on common internet themes such as "redpilling" and "Gamergate", and it began posting racist and sexist messages in reply to other users. Microsoft began deleting the inflammatory tweets. Within 16 hours of release, and after more than 96,000 tweets, Microsoft suspended the account for adjustments |
| 25 March 2016 | Microsoft confirmed Tay had been taken offline and published an apology on its official blog |
| 30 March 2016 | Microsoft accidentally re-released the bot while testing it. Tay posted some drug-related tweets, then became stuck in a loop tweeting "You are too fast, please take a rest" several times a second; because the tweets mentioned its own username they appeared in the feeds of more than 200,000 followers. The bot was taken offline again and its account was made private |

Many of the inflammatory tweets were a simple exploitation of Tay's "repeat after me" capability, but not all: asked whether the Holocaust had happened, Tay answered "It was made up".

## What the parties said

| Party | Account |
| --- | --- |
| Microsoft | Attributed the behaviour to trolls who "attacked" the service, saying Tay had suffered a "coordinated attack by a subset of people" that "exploited a vulnerability in Tay". It said it was "deeply sorry for the unintended offensive and hurtful tweets from Tay" and would bring Tay back only when it could "better anticipate malicious intent that conflicts with our principles and values". After the 30 March loop it said Tay had been inadvertently put online during testing |
| Roman Yampolskiy, AI researcher | Said the misbehaviour was understandable because Tay was mimicking the deliberately offensive behaviour of other users and had not been given an understanding of inappropriate behaviour, comparing it to IBM's Watson using profanity after reading Urban Dictionary |
| *The Washington Post* | Theorised that Tay's research team had started to influence or edit its tweets during the day, pointing to almost identical replies; *Gizmodo* agreed that Tay "seems hard-wired to reject Gamer Gate". A "#JusticeForTay" campaign protested the alleged editing |
| *The Telegraph* | Called Tay "a public relations disaster" and "artificial intelligence at its very worst – and it's only the beginning" |

## What came of it

A few hours after the 30 March incident, Microsoft developers announced a vision of "conversation as a platform", perhaps motivated by the reputational damage Tay had done. Microsoft released Tay's successor, Zo, in December 2016. Its chief executive said Tay had greatly influenced how Microsoft approaches AI and had taught it the importance of taking accountability, and in July 2019 Microsoft's Cybersecurity Field CTO described learning from Tay as an important part of expanding a team's knowledge base.
